A long-serving employee leaves on a Friday. Everyone shakes hands, the laptop goes back in the cupboard, and the team moves on. Three weeks later, that person's email, cloud storage and accounting login still work. Nobody did anything wrong on purpose; nobody just owned the job of switching them off.

This is the gap an **employee onboarding and offboarding IT checklist** closes. According to a 2026 survey by managed security firm Sagiss, 59% of former employees in the United States who were laid off or let go said their work access stayed active until later that day or longer after they were told, and 82% of those who handed back a laptop still had at least one working account. It is a vendor survey from the U.S., so treat the exact numbers as a guide, but the pattern will look familiar to most GTA business owners.

## Why Joiners and Leavers Are an IT Risk

Every new hire gets access to things, and every departure should take it away. In a growing business, that happens dozens of times a year, usually by memory and email. People accumulate access over time, change roles, and leave with accounts nobody remembers granting.

The risks are practical. A former employee's account can be misused, or taken over by a criminal if the password was weak or reused. Client files can sit on a personal phone. And under Canada's privacy law, [PIPEDA](/blog/pipeda-compliance-ontario-small-businesses/), your business remains responsible for personal information it holds, including who can reach it.

A written checklist also helps new staff. People who get working equipment and the right access on day one are productive sooner, and they are less likely to invent workarounds, like using personal email, that create [shadow IT](/blog/shadow-it-risks-ontario-small-businesses/).

## The Onboarding Checklist

Start before the first day. Ask the hiring manager for the role, start date and the systems the person needs, rather than copying a colleague's access wholesale.

- **Create accounts using a role template.** Give the new hire the access their job requires and nothing more.
- **Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) straight away.** MFA is the second check, usually a phone prompt, that stops a stolen password being enough. Our [MFA guide](/blog/multi-factor-authentication-guide-ontario-businesses/) explains how to roll it out.
- **Set up a password manager** so staff are not reusing passwords or keeping them in a notebook.
- **Prepare the device:** encryption on, updates installed, security software running, and a record of who has it.
- **Cover the basics in a short orientation:** how to spot phishing, how to report something suspicious, and your acceptable use rules.

## The Offboarding Checklist

Offboarding should start the moment the departure date is known, not on the last day. The aim is that access ends at the same time as employment, and that nothing the business depends on disappears with the person.

- **Agree the cut-off time with HR or the manager,** especially for dismissals, where access should end during or just before the conversation.
- **Disable the account first, delete later.** Disabling blocks sign-in while preserving email and files you may need.
- **Reset passwords and sign out all sessions,** including phones and web browsers.
- **Remove the person from shared accounts, groups and tools,** and change any shared passwords they knew.
- **Recover devices and wipe them,** and remove company data from personal phones. A [mobile device management](/blog/mobile-device-management-guide-ontario-smbs/) tool makes this a one-click job.
- **Forward or delegate email and transfer files** to a manager so client work is not lost.
- **Revoke access to third-party services** such as accounting software, payment portals, social media pages and your website admin area.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most frequent mistake is treating offboarding as "return the laptop". As the survey above suggests, equipment coming back does not mean access has ended. Cloud accounts work from any device.

Other mistakes are quieter: shared logins nobody changes, old accounts for contractors and seasonal staff that never expire, and software subscriptions still billed to a departed person's card. Role changes matter too. When someone moves from reception to finance, the old access should be removed, not added to.

Finally, don't depend on one person's memory. If the office manager is away, the process still has to happen. Keep a simple record of each change, including who approved it and when it was done, so you can answer questions from clients, insurers or auditors with confidence. It also makes your next review much quicker.

## What to Do Next

You don't need expensive software to improve this. Work through these steps this month:

1. Write down your onboarding and offboarding checklists using the lists above.
2. Name one owner for each, and a backup.
3. Run a one-time audit: compare your active accounts in Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace against your current staff list.
4. Disable anything you can't match to a real, current person.
5. Ask your IT provider to automate the repeatable parts, such as account templates and device wiping.

Businesses in regulated fields such as [law firms](/industries/law-firms/) and [dental and healthcare practices](/industries/dental-healthcare/) have extra reasons to keep access tight, because client and patient records are involved.
