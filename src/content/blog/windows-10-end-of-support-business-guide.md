Somewhere in your office there is probably a computer that "still works fine." It boots quickly, runs your accounting package and nobody has complained. It also runs Windows 10, and that is the problem. **Windows 10 end of support** arrived on October 14, 2025, and according to StatCounter's browser-based estimates, roughly 28% of Windows computers worldwide were still running it as of September 2026. If you are one of those businesses, this guide explains what that means and what to do about it, without the jargon.

## What "end of support" actually means

Your computers do not stop working. What stops is the flow of free security updates, bug fixes and technical help from Microsoft. Think of it like a vehicle recall system that has been switched off: the car still drives, but when a new defect is found, nobody is coming to fix it.

Attackers know this. Every month Microsoft publishes fixes for newly discovered weaknesses. On supported versions of Windows, those fixes arrive automatically. On Windows 10 without extra coverage, the weaknesses become permanent, and criminals can study the published fixes for Windows 11 to work out how to attack the unpatched older systems.

The risk grows with time. A machine that was acceptable in November 2025 is noticeably more exposed a year later, which is why this is worth acting on now rather than at your next hardware refresh.

## The Extended Security Updates option, and its limits

Microsoft offers paid Extended Security Updates (ESU) as a bridge. According to Microsoft's published pricing, as reported by [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/software-apps/windows-10/windows-10-extended-support-pricing-revealed), commercial ESU starts at US$61 per device for the first year and doubles each year, to a maximum of three years. Commercial coverage runs to October 13, 2026 (year one), October 12, 2027 (year two) and October 10, 2028 (year three). Confirm current pricing and your enrolment window with your Microsoft licensing provider, because Canadian quotes differ.

Two things are worth knowing before you rely on it:

- **ESU covers critical and important security fixes only.** There are no new features, no general bug fixes and no technical support.
- **The price doubles.** For a 20-computer office, a second year costs more than most businesses expect, and the money buys you time, not a solution.

For most small businesses, ESU is best used as a short, deliberate bridge for the few machines you cannot replace yet, not as a long-term plan.

## Why this matters for Ontario businesses

Running unsupported software can undermine commitments you have already made. Under Canada's privacy law, PIPEDA, organisations must protect personal information with safeguards appropriate to its sensitivity. If a breach traces back to a machine that had no security updates for a year, that is a difficult position to defend. See our guide to [PIPEDA compliance for Ontario small businesses](/blog/pipeda-compliance-ontario-small-businesses/) for the wider picture.

Insurers are paying attention too. Cyber insurance applications commonly ask whether you run unsupported operating systems, and a "yes" can mean higher premiums or a declined claim. Our [cyber insurance guide](/blog/cyber-insurance-guide-ontario-small-businesses/) covers what underwriters look for.

Regulated practices carry extra weight. A dental or medical office handling patient records, for example, should treat an unsupported computer as a compliance issue, not just an IT one. ## A practical plan, step by step

You do not need to replace everything in a weekend. Work through it in this order:

1. **Count what you have.** List every computer, who uses it and what version of Windows it runs. Right-click Start, choose System, and check the Windows version. A proper inventory, as described in our [IT asset management guide](/blog/it-asset-management-guide-ontario-smbs/), makes this faster.
2. **Check whether each machine can run Windows 11.** Microsoft requires specific hardware, including a TPM 2.0 security chip and a supported processor. Many computers bought before about 2019 do not qualify.
3. **Upgrade the eligible ones first.** If a computer meets the requirements, the upgrade is free and often takes under an hour per machine. Back up first.
4. **Replace the rest in priority order.** Start with machines that handle money, client data or email, and leave the least critical for last.
5. **Isolate what must stay.** If old software forces you to keep a Windows 10 machine, remove its email and web browsing duties, restrict its network access and enrol it in ESU.

## What to do next

This week, build the list of Windows 10 machines. Next, decide which ones upgrade, which get replaced and which need a short ESU bridge, and set dates for each. Keep [patching](/blog/patch-management-guide-ontario-smbs/) and endpoint protection current on everything in the meantime, since [modern endpoint detection](/blog/edr-vs-antivirus-ontario-small-business/) adds a layer of defence while older systems wait their turn.

Budget matters, so spread replacements across a quarter or two rather than buying everything at once. Many businesses find a planned rollout costs less than emergency replacement after a failure, and it avoids downtime during your busy season.
